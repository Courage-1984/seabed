The Solder Over Silicon Valley Doctrine: Electronics Built to Be Mended

High up in the Moelwyn range, when winter storms push freezing cloud through the passes, modern surface-mount electronics give up the ghost. Microscopic solder joints crack under thermal shock, proprietary sealed batteries swell into useless bricks, and discarded plastic enclosures shatter against granite crags. When cellular towers down in the valleys lose mains power during heavy ice accumulation, entire upland watersheds vanish into communications blackouts.

We take a stubborn, craft-led approach to network engineering. Inside our water-powered stone workshop at Cwmorthin, we build communications nodes using generous through-hole components, discrete power regulators, and beefy wide-pitch copper circuit traces that anyone with a battery-powered soldering iron can diagnose and repair on a windswept ridge. We reject glued assemblies; our chassis are machined from off-cut quarry slate and native oak, bolted together with marine-grade stainless hardware and sealed with unrefined Welsh beeswax.

These machines do not depend on distant corporate data centres or proprietary cellular contracts. Operating on open amateur and ISM radio bands, they form self-healing store-and-forward networks that hop telemetry packets across thirty miles of jagged ridgelines on fractions of a single watt. It is honest, resilient technology designed to live out on the cold mountain alongside the ravens and the feral goats for decades.
