Metallurgical Integrity of High-Nickel Invar Alloys

Why thermodynamic thermal welding inevitably ruins grey cast iron, while controlled cold-mechanical plastic swaging restores full structural load capacity.

The catastrophic brittleness of grey cast iron stems from its internal micro-structure: randomly distributed, sharp-edged flakes of free crystalline graphite embedded within a pearlitic iron matrix. Under electric arc welding exceeding 1,200°C, the localized thermal influx causes carbon from the graphite flakes to dissolve into the liquid weld puddle. Upon rapid cooling through the surrounding cold mass of the heavy casting, this carbon transforms into untempered martensite and massive iron carbide (Fe₃C) platelets with a hardness exceeding 600 Vickers. This hard, unyielding boundary creates severe internal stresses that inevitably trigger secondary stress-corrosion fractures alongside the heat-affected zone.

The Govan cold-stitching specification bypasses thermal recrystallization by employing a proprietary 60% nickel and 40% iron Invar formulation. This custom alloy exhibits an exceptionally low coefficient of thermal expansion (matching grey iron at 10.5 × 10⁻⁶ /°C between 20°C and 300°C) coupled with 35% elongation at failure. When cold-peened pneumatically into machined keyways, the alloy work-hardens within the cavity without creating internal stress risers, locking the crack faces together with permanent compressive resilience.

Micro-Phase I

Pearlite-Graphite Lamellae in Host Casting

Grade 14 grey iron (BS 1452) typically contains 3.2% to 3.5% total carbon, with the vast majority precipitated as Type A flake graphite arranged in rosette groupings within fine lamellar pearlite. These graphite flakes act as natural micro-notches, giving grey iron its superior acoustic damping capacity but negligible tensile ductility.

When cyclic marine propulsion torque causes a crack to advance along these graphite boundaries, no plastic yield occurs ahead of the crack tip. The fissure cleaves cleanly through the matrix. The Govan method respects this micro-structure by avoiding heat, which would otherwise burn out carbon flakes and produce gas porosity and brittle ledeburite.

  • Carbon Structure:Type A Flake Graphite (ASTM A247)
  • Matrix Micro-Constituent:100% Fine Lamellar Pearlite
  • Tensile Elastic Limit:0.5% Maximum Strain Prior to Fracture
Micro-Phase II

Invar-60 Tensile Key Geometry & Micro-Alloying

Our cold-stitching keys are precision-broached from hot-rolled bars of FeNi36 modified with 1.8% manganese and 0.25% silicon to optimize cold strain hardening. In its annealed state, this face-centred cubic austenitic alloy displays a tensile yield of 240 N/mm² and exceptional elongation of 35%.

The serrated multi-lobe geometry is engineered with a five-degree inward taper on each shoulder. As the key is seated into the transverse slot, this taper creates an inward clamping force that draws the fractured iron walls together before the first hammer blow is struck.

  • Nickel Content:59.8% – 61.2% High Purity Electrolytic
  • Crystalline Structure:Ductile Austenitic FCC Solid Solution
  • Pre-Peening Tensile Elongation:35% Minimum in 50mm Gauge
Micro-Phase III

Pneumatic Radial Swaging & Grain Displacement

During the pneumatic hammering cycle, high-intensity mechanical stress waves induce localized dislocation tangles within the austenitic Invar lattice. The key alloy undergoes work-hardening, elevating its hardness from 140 HV to over 285 HV directly at the interface zone.

Simultaneously, the ductile Invar flows plastically into the microscopic irregularities of the milled cast-iron keyway wall. At 500× magnification, no void or gap is detectable between the alloys. The radial expansion generates a continuous compressive pre-stress of 180 N/mm² across the fracture faces, permanently arresting tensile opening.

  • Work-Hardened Hardness:285–310 HV10 Post-Swaging
  • Interface Interlock:Microscopic Mechanical Interference
  • Residual Compressive Pre-Stress:180 N/mm² Transverse Bias
Micro-Phase IV

Gas-Tight Hydrostatic Seal via Stud Lacing

The final metallurgical stage introduces overlapping threaded studs between the transverse keys along the fracture trace. Formed from a specialized copper-nickel alloy (CuNi10Fe1Mn), these studs undergo torsional yield during insertion, shearing off at their neck reliefs to leave an interference core.

As adjacent studs are stepped through preceding threads by 40% diametral pitch, the softer copper-nickel element cold-swages against the iron threads, creating a continuous labyrinth seal impervious to superheated steam, heavy fuel oil, and pressurized sea water.

  • Lacing Alloy:CuNi10Fe1Mn Phosphor-Doped Copper-Nickel
  • Thread Overlap Ratio:40% Diametral Step-Through
  • Hermetic Seal Proof:Zero Helium Leakage at 35 bar Differential

NDT Validation · Section IV

Classification Society Inspection Standards

Every cold-stitched marine casting undergoes a mandatory three-tier non-destructive testing (NDT) regimen before classification society surveyors from Lloyd’s Register, DNV, or Bureau Veritas will certify the vessel for ocean service. Because cast iron exhibits naturally high acoustic damping, standard high-angle ultrasonic testing must be paired with magnetic particle and resonant timbre verification.

1. Wet Fluorescent Magnetic Particle Inspection (MPI)

Under high-amperage electromagnetic yoke excitation (1,200 A AC), fluorescent iron oxide particles in kerosene carrier are misted across the degreased casting face. Under 365nm ultraviolet illumination in a darkened mobile enclosure, sub-surface fissure trajectories and hairline bifurcations glow sharp emerald, establishing absolute boundary control for drill stops.

2. Ultrasonic Angle-Beam Subsurface Probing

Dual-crystal 4MHz shear-wave probes angled at 45 and 60 degrees evaluate the depth of the fracture through wall sections up to 150mm. Ultrasonic attenuation confirms that the multi-lobed keyways have penetrated 75% of the cast wall without encroaching on internal cooling passages or combustion liners.

3. The Traditional Clyde Hammer Timbre Ring Test

A hallmark of Clydeside foundry craftsmanship codified into survey procedure: a 1.5-lb copper-headed testing hammer strikes the casting adjacent to the stitched seam. A fractured casting produces a dull, dead clack indicative of energy dissipation across a void. A properly cold-stitched web emits a clear, sustained bell-like metallic chime across 850 Hz, proving monolithic acoustic resonance has been restored.

Hydrostatic Rigour · Section V

High-Pressure Steam & Hydraulic Sealing Rigour

Marine engine cylinder blocks and reciprocating pump volutes operate under relentless cyclic hydraulic and steam pressure. Sealing a cracked casting without thermal weld fusion demands a cold-worked metal-to-metal interference fit capable of resisting both fluid permeation and differential expansion.

Following keyway swaging and overlapping stud lacing, the repaired component is sealed with steel blanking flanges and torqued to working pressure. Cold water treated with a fluorescent dye penetrant is pumped into the chamber until proof pressure—typically 1.5 times the maximum rated operating pressure (up to 35 bar)—is achieved. The casting remains under continuous hydrostatic hold for 48 hours while dial test indicators monitor for microscopic dimensional creep.

Because the copper-nickel studs are cold-worked directly into the iron threads, no gaskets, polymeric epoxies, or liquid chemical sealants are utilized. The resulting labyrinth joint maintains its hermetic integrity across steam temperature gradients ranging from 20°C in port to 280°C under full superheated engine load.
